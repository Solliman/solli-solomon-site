THE WEIGHT OF APPEARANCE



In the Gospel of Matthew 23 an emblematic figure emerges: the Pharisee who takes the first place in the synagogue, praying for show with a heart full of pride, considering himself better than others. This image could represent anyone, even without being aware of it. 

There is a real danger in focusing on the mistakes of others – whether a spouse, parents, siblings, friends, or the people around us – while neglecting our own inner condition.

Authentic Christianity is not founded on an idea of human perfection. On the contrary, its core is unconditional love. It is not a collection of rules, precepts and rituals to obtain blessings: that is a distortion of the true Gospel. Whoever believes they are spiritually advanced, theologically prepared or the keeper of special revelations should show an attitude of greater love and compassion. Instead, all too often this supposed superiority turns into religious fanaticism that judges and condemns.

The essence of the Christian message lies precisely in this: God loved humanity while it was still immersed in sin.

Romans 5:8 – "But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us."

The most obvious paradox lies in how differently sins are regarded. Why are some mistakes, usually the most visible ones, branded with particular severity, while others, just as serious but less conspicuous, are silently tolerated? If we wanted to apply a consistent standard of judgment, every transgression should be weighed with the same severity. In reality, we often adopt a human scale of values that has little to do with God's perspective.

This discrepancy turns into discriminatory practices: those who show "visible" sins (such as smoking, having tattoos, living together) are sometimes denied baptism, access to the Lord's Supper or roles of service in the community. At the same time, those who cultivate "invisible" sins (pride, gossip, falsehood, greed) but keep an irreproachable appearance carry on undisturbed, taking part in every church activity and often even holding positions of responsibility.

A glaring example of this inconsistency is gluttony. In the Bible it is placed on the same level as drunkenness and lust (Luke 21:34, Proverbs 23:20-21), and yet it is simply ignored. While some sins are condemned immediately, excess in food is accepted without a problem, as if it were not an equally serious transgression. This shows once again how much human judgment is shaped more by cultural habits than by real faithfulness to the Holy Scriptures, which also invite believers to keep a balanced diet.

Jesus himself harshly criticized this hypocrisy, condemning those who "strain out a gnat but swallow a camel" (Matthew 23:24)

This mindset not only betrays the spirit of the Gospel, it ends up hindering the work of the Holy Spirit. Excessive trust in human rules and structures, presented as absolute truths but lacking a solid scriptural foundation, risks turning faith into a sectarian system, far from the freedom and love that should define it.

Faith becomes a religion of checks, bans and merits instead of a living relationship with God based on love and grace. The emphasis on certain outward sins at the expense of inward ones reveals how appearance counts more than the heart, exactly as it did with the Pharisees of Jesus' time.

Let us remember that God looks at the heart, not at appearances. He loves us just as we are and wants to help us change. But true change is born from his love, not from fear or from feeling judged. 

This is the authentic Gospel!

Thank you for taking the time to read this.
